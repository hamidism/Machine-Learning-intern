Refresh Priority Scoring: A Content Opportunity Model for Search Portfolios

Comparing a hand-written scoring rule against a validated machine learning model for prioritizing content review, using one month of production search data.

Abstract

Summary

This study asks a narrow, practical question: among the pages in a content portfolio, which ones should a reviewer with limited time look at first? Using six page-level features — content depth, backlinks, position, and a CTR-vs-position gap — a Random Forest classifier is trained to rank pages by decline risk and compared against a simple hand-written rule on the same held-out clients. Under an honest, client-grouped validation split, the model reaches Precision@50 of 0.64 against the rule's 0.48, a real but modest gain, while a naive random split on the identical data inflates that same number to 0.90 purely through client leakage. The paper reports both results deliberately, alongside a feature-importance and error analysis showing where the model's blind spots are, and converts the validated output into a ranked, reason-coded action queue with explicit human-review and no-automation rules. The result is a decision-support tool, not a proof of causation — its claims are observed and directional, scoped to one mid-panel month of one dataset slice.

2

Introduction

A content or SEO reviewer working through a large page portfolio cannot manually audit every page every cycle. Their real constraint is capacity — typically the top 20 to 100 candidates per review cycle — not comprehensiveness. The question this study answers is not "is this page declining?" in the abstract, but "given limited time, which pages should be opened first?"

That framing sets the cost structure that shapes every choice in this paper. A false positive — a page flagged as worth reviewing that turns out fine — costs a few minutes of a reviewer's time. A false negative — a genuinely declining, high-visibility page that never gets flagged — costs silent, compounding traffic loss, because nobody was ever told to look. The scoring approach and the evaluation metric used throughout (Precision@K, not accuracy) both follow from this asymmetry.

Formally, this is treated as a ranking / scoring problem: the output is an ordered queue with a probability-based score, not a single binary label, because getting the relative order right near the top of the list matters more than classifying every page correctly.

Data

This study uses the FlyRank ML Internship warehouse, a release of de-identified, brand-anonymized production search performance data spanning January 2025 through June 2026. The unit of analysis throughout is one page (content_hash_id), aggregated over a single mid-panel month.

March 2026 was chosen deliberately as a mid-panel month rather than the dataset's final month. The release's _sample table corresponds exactly to June 2026 — the natural outcome window for any past-to-future label — and was reserved as a sealed test month, never used to develop label logic.

Filtering and exclusions

Rows were kept only where gsc_data_available and ga4_data_available were both true, and pages were required to have at least 50 total impressions in the month to avoid scoring near-zero-traffic pages on noise. This reduced the working slice from 9.8 million page-days to 61,125 candidate pages, and the final modeling set, after joining static attributes and dropping incomplete rows, to 26,562 pages.

Two fields were deliberately excluded from the feature set:

  • provider_used, model_used — describe how content was produced, not how it performs; including them risks learning tool-brand artifacts rather than genuine content-quality signal.
  • Channel-level session breakdowns (paid, social, referral, AI) — omitted to keep the feature set small and interpretable rather than exhaustive.
Data quality finding

content_updated_date could not be used as a reliable staleness signal. Eighty percent of pages carry an update date after the March 2026 analysis window (maximum date: 2026-07-06), meaning that information was not knowable at decision time. Within the smaller valid slice, 11,359 pages — 18.6 percent of the entire dataset — share the exact same value, far too precise to reflect organic edit history. Both observations point to this field recording a bulk pipeline event rather than per-page editing history. It was excluded from scoring on this basis, and is reported here as a limitation rather than worked around silently.

Methodology

Label definition

The target, referred to here as the decline label, is a rule-derived proxy rather than an independently observed outcome: a page is labeled positive if its second-half-of-March impressions are lower than its first-half impressions, and negative otherwise. This is a defined comparison, not a confirmed causal decline, and that distinction is carried through every claim in this paper.

Features

Table 2. Features used in the model, and why each is knowable at decision time.
FeatureRationale
word_count, char_countStatic content properties, set at publish time
backlinksA running historical count as of report time, not a future value
avg_positionMeasured from March's already-occurred search impressions
ctr_gapClick-through rate relative to the mean for the page's position tier
search_volumeStatic keyword-level property

Baseline

The baseline ranks pages by ctr_gap alone — how far a page's click-through rate falls short of the average for pages at the same position tier. This signal was verified before being trusted: weighted click-through rate falls cleanly from 3.5 percent at positions 1–3 to 1.2 percent at positions 21–50, confirming the expected relationship. A second candidate signal, content staleness, was tested and rejected: given the date-quality issue described above, it could only be computed for 19.8 percent of pages, and showed no reliable relationship with decline in that reduced sample.

Model and validation design

A Random Forest classifier (200 trees, maximum depth 8, class-balanced) was selected for the modeling stage. The features have non-linear, interacting relationships with the label — a given CTR gap means something different at position 3 than at position 40 — which a tree ensemble captures without manually specified interaction terms.

Validation used a client-grouped split (70/30) rather than a random row-level split. Pages from the same client share client-specific baselines that a random split would let the model partly memorize, rather than learning content signal that generalizes to clients it has not seen. The grouped split was verified directly to have zero client overlap between the training and test sets.

The model improves on the baseline at every value of k tested, most notably at k=100 (0.43 to 0.70), suggesting it is particularly useful for surfacing good candidates further down a longer review queue, not only at the very top.

Why validation design matters

The same model, trained on identical data but evaluated with a naive random split instead of a client-grouped one, reported Precision@20/50/100 of 0.95 / 0.90 / 0.88, with 20 clients appearing in both the training and test sets. That is roughly an eighteen-to-thirty-five point inflation from split design alone, with no change to the model or the features. The grouped-split figures reported above (0.60 / 0.64 / 0.70) are the honest result, and the one this paper stands behind.

Content-depth signals carried more weight than expected at the outset of this study — more, in fact, than the CTR-versus-position gap that anchored the baseline rule.

Error analysis

Reviewing the true decliners the model ranked lowest reveals a consistent pattern: strong existing position (mostly top three), a near-zero CTR gap, low search volume, and largely zero backlinks. The model reads "ranks well, click-through rate looks fine" as healthy, even when a page is genuinely declining by the label, because none of the six features flag a problem. A reviewer relying on this model alone would likely miss slow, low-volume erosion that does not show up in a single-month position or CTR snapshot.

Leakage audit

avg_position and ctr_gap are computed from the full March window, which technically overlaps the second half of the month used in the label. Checked directly, the full-month ctr_gap's correlation with the label (0.067) is weaker, not stronger, than a first-half-only proxy's correlation (−0.153) — unlike a deliberately injected leak examined earlier in this project, which pushed correlation sharply higher. The theoretical overlap does not appear to translate into meaningful practical leakage here, though a stricter production pipeline would still compute every feature from the first half of the month alone.

Limitations and Honest Framing

This work supports

  • Observed correlations between content signals and refresh priority, in this dataset, this month.
  • Measured improvement of a learned model over a hand-written rule, on held-out clients.
  • Directional, decision-support output — a ranked queue indicating where to look first.

This work does not support

  • Any claim that refreshing a flagged page will cause recovery.
  • Any claim about how a search engine's ranking algorithm works.
  • Generalization beyond March 2026; the model is untested on other months.

A Precision@50 of 0.64 also means roughly one in three of the top fifty flagged pages may still be a false positive on unseen clients. This is a queue meant for review, not a queue meant to be acted on unread.

Required human review

  • Does the reason code hold up when the page is opened manually — is the CTR gap real, or is impression volume too low to trust it?
  • Is there a non-content explanation for the decline — seasonality, an external algorithm shift, a redirect issue — that a refresh would not fix?
  • Is the page tied to a live campaign or compliance-sensitive content requiring approval beyond the content team?

What should not be automated

  • Publishing any content change from this score alone; every action requires a human editor.
  • Treating a "healthy" flag as permanent — it reflects a snapshot, not a guarantee, and monitoring should continue regardless.
  • Using this score as an input to billing, contract terms, or any consequence beyond internal workflow prioritization.

Monitoring and retraining triggers

The recommendations above should be re-run and the model retrained when any of the following occur:

  • On a routine monthly basis, since the model was validated on March 2026 only.
  • Precision@50 on a new month drops more than roughly ten points below 0.64.
  • The AUC drops meaningfully below 0.648, signaling degraded overall ranking ability.
  • The rate of declining pages moves far outside the roughly 37 percent observed in March.
  • A wave of new clients has no representation in the training set.
